## SECTION A — TAINTS AND TOLERATIONS

---

### Q1. What is a taint and what is a toleration? How do they work together?

**Answer:**

> "A taint is a marker placed on a NODE that repels pods by default. It's like a 'Restricted Entry' sign on a door — by default, nobody gets in. A toleration is a marker placed on a POD that exempts it from a specific taint — like having the right access badge to enter that restricted door.
>
> They work as a pair. You put a taint on a node and all pods without a matching toleration cannot be scheduled there. Then you add tolerations to specific pods that legitimately need to run on that tainted node.
>
> Real example: we had dedicated GPU nodes for machine learning workloads. We tainted them with `nvidia.com/gpu=true:NoSchedule`. This prevented regular web server pods and microservice pods from landing on expensive GPU instances. Only our ML training pods, which had the matching toleration, could schedule there. Regular pods couldn't get there even if every other node was full — they'd stay Pending instead of wasting GPU resources."

---

### Q2. What are the three taint effects? How are they different?

**Answer:**

> "The three effects control how strongly the taint repels pods.
>
> NoSchedule is a hard rule for new pods. Pods without a matching toleration cannot be scheduled on this node. But pods that are ALREADY running on this node when the taint is added continue running — they're not evicted. This is the most common effect for dedicated node pools.
>
> PreferNoSchedule is soft. The scheduler tries to avoid placing pods here without a matching toleration, but if no other suitable node exists, it can schedule there anyway. It's a hint, not a hard rule. I use this when I want to reserve nodes but not strictly exclude.
>
> NoExecute is the strongest effect. It does everything NoSchedule does — prevents new pods — but also EVICTS existing pods that don't have a matching toleration. When you add a NoExecute taint to a running node, all pods without tolerations are immediately evicted and rescheduled elsewhere. Kubernetes uses NoExecute internally — when a node goes NotReady, Kubernetes automatically adds `node.kubernetes.io/not-ready:NoExecute` to evict pods after 5 minutes."

---

### Q3. ⚠️ What does `tolerationSeconds` do in a NoExecute toleration?

**Answer:**

> "tolerationSeconds adds a time limit to how long a pod will tolerate a NoExecute taint before being evicted.
>
> Without tolerationSeconds: the pod tolerates the NoExecute taint forever — it will NEVER be evicted due to that taint.
>
> With `tolerationSeconds: 3600`: the pod can stay on the node for 3600 seconds (1 hour) after the NoExecute taint is added. After that hour, if the taint is still there, the pod is evicted.
>
> This is used for graceful handling of node problems. Kubernetes automatically adds two NoExecute taints when a node has issues: `node.kubernetes.io/not-ready` and `node.kubernetes.io/unreachable`. All pods have a default toleration for these with `tolerationSeconds: 300` (5 minutes). This means if a node goes down, your pods wait 5 minutes before being rescheduled to another node — giving time for transient network issues to resolve without unnecessary pod movements. You can change this grace period by setting different tolerationSeconds on your pods."

---

### Q4. ⚠️ A pod has a toleration for a taint. Does it HAVE to go to the tainted node?

**Answer:**

> "No — and this is the most common misunderstanding about taints and tolerations.
>
> A toleration gives the pod PERMISSION to be scheduled on a tainted node. It does NOT mean the pod will be scheduled there. The pod can still be scheduled on any other node that passes filtering and scoring.
>
> Think of it as having a VIP pass to a nightclub. Having the pass doesn't mean you must go to that nightclub. You could still choose another bar. The pass just means you're not turned away at the door if you show up there.
>
> To FORCE a pod to go to a tainted node, you need BOTH a toleration (permission to be there) AND node affinity (a requirement or preference to go there). Toleration alone is insufficient.
>
> In our GPU example: the ML training pod had a toleration for `nvidia.com/gpu=true:NoSchedule` AND a required node affinity for the `accelerator=nvidia-tesla-v100` label. Without both, the pod would be allowed on GPU nodes but might still end up on a regular node because it doesn't prefer GPU nodes."

---

### Q5. How do DaemonSet pods get scheduled on tainted nodes?

**Answer:**

> "DaemonSets are designed to run on EVERY node — that's their entire purpose. But if nodes have taints, the DaemonSet pods would be blocked by those taints.
>
> Kubernetes automatically adds tolerations to DaemonSet pods for several critical system taints. DaemonSet pods automatically tolerate `node.kubernetes.io/not-ready`, `node.kubernetes.io/unreachable`, `node.kubernetes.io/disk-pressure`, `node.kubernetes.io/memory-pressure`, `node.kubernetes.io/unschedulable`, and `node.kubernetes.io/network-unavailable`. This ensures monitoring agents, log collectors, and networking plugins (like Calico or Cilium) continue running even on troubled nodes.
>
> For user-defined taints — like our `dedicated=banking:NoSchedule` on banking nodes — DaemonSets do NOT automatically get tolerations. If you have a DaemonSet like Fluentd that must run on all nodes including your tainted ones, you must explicitly add the matching toleration to the DaemonSet's pod spec. Without it, the DaemonSet won't schedule pods on tainted nodes and you'll have logging blind spots."

## SECTION B — NODE SELECTOR AND NODE AFFINITY

---

### Q6. What is the difference between NodeSelector and Node Affinity?

**Answer:**

> "Both control which nodes a pod can run on based on node labels. NodeSelector is simple; Node Affinity is powerful.
>
> NodeSelector is a flat key-value map. The node must have ALL listed labels. It's binary — either the node matches all labels and the pod can go there, or it doesn't and the pod stays Pending. No OR logic, no NOT logic, no preferences, no weights.
>
> Node Affinity supports operators — In, NotIn, Exists, DoesNotExist, Gt, Lt. It supports OR logic through multiple nodeSelectorTerms (pod can go to zone A OR zone B). It supports NOT logic through NotIn operator (not in dev or test environments). Most importantly, it has two modes: required (hard) and preferred (soft with weights).
>
> The preferred mode is what NodeSelector completely lacks. With preferred affinity, you say 'I'd like to go to an SSD node but if none are available, any node is fine.' The weight (1-100) lets you prioritize among multiple preferences. NodeSelector cannot do this — it's all-or-nothing.
>
> When to use which: NodeSelector for simple cases where you just need 'put this on a GPU node' and there's no ambiguity. Node Affinity for anything more complex — multi-AZ scheduling, dev/prod separation, soft preferences, NOT conditions."

---

### Q7. ⚠️ Explain the OR and AND logic in Node Affinity. How does nodeSelectorTerms relate to matchExpressions?

**Answer:**

> "This is critical to get right because the logic is counterintuitive if you don't know where OR and AND apply.
>
> nodeSelectorTerms is a list. Multiple items in this list are combined with OR. If you have two nodeSelectorTerms, the pod can go to a node that matches EITHER term. This gives you OR between different sets of requirements.
>
> matchExpressions within a single nodeSelectorTerm is a list of conditions combined with AND. The node must satisfy ALL conditions in a single matchExpressions list.
>
> Concrete example: pod must go to (zone us-east-1a AND node-type compute) OR (zone us-west-2a AND node-type memory). You write this as two separate nodeSelectorTerms, each with its own matchExpressions containing two conditions. The pod satisfies the requirement if it matches either complete term.
>
> The easy mnemonic: terms are OR, expressions within a term are AND. Many people write a single term when they meant OR logic between different sets of requirements, and their pod ends up Pending because the single term's AND conditions can't all be met simultaneously."

---

### Q8. What does `IgnoredDuringExecution` mean in Node Affinity? Is there a `RequiredDuringExecution`?

**Answer:**

> "IgnoredDuringExecution means the affinity rules are only checked at SCHEDULING TIME — when the pod is first being placed on a node. If the node's labels change after the pod is already running, Kubernetes ignores the change and the pod continues running undisturbed.
>
> For example: pod has required affinity for `environment=production` label. It schedules on a production node. Someone then removes that label from the node. With IgnoredDuringExecution, the pod keeps running. The node label change is ignored for already-running pods.
>
> RequiredDuringExecution would mean: if the node stops matching the affinity rule after the pod is running, the pod gets evicted. This is more strict. As of current Kubernetes versions, RequiredDuringExecution is NOT yet stable or fully implemented. The full name `requiredDuringSchedulingRequiredDuringExecution` appears in the API spec but isn't enforced yet.
>
> In practice this means all current Node Affinity only applies at scheduling time. If you need to enforce node conditions on running pods, you'd use Taints and Tolerations instead — adding a NoExecute taint achieves the running-pod eviction that RequiredDuringExecution would do."

---

### Q9. ⚠️ When would you use both Taints/Tolerations AND Node Affinity together?

**Answer:**

> "Taints/Tolerations and Node Affinity solve different sides of the same problem, and you often need both together for complete control.
>
> Taints prevent UNWANTED pods from going to a node. Tolerations give permission to go there. Node Affinity ATTRACTS pods to specific nodes. They complement each other.
>
> Classic scenario: dedicated GPU nodes. First, taint the GPU nodes with `nvidia.com/gpu=true:NoSchedule`. This prevents all regular pods from landing there — the nodes are reserved. Then, add a toleration for that taint on ML training pods — they have permission to go to GPU nodes. Finally, add required node affinity for the GPU node label on ML training pods — they are attracted specifically to GPU nodes.
>
> Without the taint: any pod could accidentally land on GPU nodes, wasting expensive resources.
> Without the toleration: ML pods can't get onto GPU nodes even though they need to.
> Without the node affinity: ML pods COULD go to GPU nodes but might still schedule on regular nodes. You lose the guarantee.
>
> All three together: regular pods are repelled from GPU nodes, ML pods are allowed onto GPU nodes and actually prefer them. This is the complete solution."

## SECTION C — POD AFFINITY AND ANTI-AFFINITY

---

### Q10. What is Pod Affinity? Give a real use case.

**Answer:**

> "Pod Affinity tells the scheduler to place a pod ON THE SAME topology domain (node, zone, etc.) as pods that match a label selector. It's about co-locating related pods.
>
> The key field is `topologyKey` — it defines what 'same' means. `kubernetes.io/hostname` means same physical node. `topology.kubernetes.io/zone` means same availability zone.
>
> Real use case: a web application and its Redis cache. The app makes hundreds of Redis calls per request. If they're on different nodes, each Redis call crosses a network hop — adds latency. With Pod Affinity, you ensure the app pod lands on the same node as its Redis pod. Network calls are now localhost — microsecond latency instead of millisecond.
>
> Another use case: cost optimization. Cross-AZ traffic in AWS costs money. If your frontend pods and backend pods are in different AZs, every request crosses AZ boundaries. Using pod affinity to keep frontend and backend in the same AZ eliminates this cost.
>
> In our banking project, we used zone-level pod affinity to keep our payment service pods in the same AZ as the database replica they'd connect to. This eliminated cross-AZ latency on the database connection pool."

---

### Q11. ⚠️ What is Pod Anti-Affinity and why is it the standard for high availability?

**Answer:**

> "Pod Anti-Affinity tells the scheduler to place a pod ON A DIFFERENT topology domain from pods that match a label selector. It spreads pods apart.
>
> The most important use case is high availability for deployments. If you have 3 replicas of a payment service and no anti-affinity rules, the scheduler might put all 3 on the same node for convenience. If that node goes down — all 3 pods die simultaneously — complete service outage.
>
> With required Pod Anti-Affinity using `topologyKey: kubernetes.io/hostname` and a selector for `app: payment` — each payment pod MUST be on a different node. If only 2 nodes exist and you try to run 3 replicas, the third pod stays Pending — the anti-affinity prevents it from violating the rule. But you get a clear signal that you need more nodes.
>
> For multi-AZ HA: use `topologyKey: topology.kubernetes.io/zone` — replicas spread across zones. Even if an entire availability zone fails, you have replicas in other zones.
>
> The preferred version (soft) is useful when you want to TRY to spread but not fail if you can't. A 6-replica deployment on 3 nodes might use preferred anti-affinity — ideally 2 per node, but if one node has 3 it's acceptable.
>
> In our banking project, ALL critical services had required anti-affinity at node level and preferred anti-affinity at zone level. Single-node failures caused zero downtime."

---

### Q12. ⚠️ What is the `topologyKey` in Pod Affinity and what values can it take?

**Answer:**

> "topologyKey defines the scope — what 'co-location' or 'separation' means. It references a node label that groups nodes into topology domains.
>
> `kubernetes.io/hostname` — each node has a unique hostname, so each node is its own domain. Affinity with this key means same physical node. Anti-affinity means different physical nodes. This is what you use for spreading replicas across machines.
>
> `topology.kubernetes.io/zone` — nodes in the same AZ have the same zone label. In AWS, all nodes in us-east-1a share this label value. Affinity here means same AZ. Anti-affinity means different AZs. This is for multi-AZ HA.
>
> `topology.kubernetes.io/region` — nodes in the same region share a region label. Rarely used for affinity — most apps want zone-level granularity.
>
> Custom keys — you can create your own topology by labeling nodes with custom keys. For example, `rack=rack-1` on nodes in the same physical rack. Pod anti-affinity with this key ensures pods are on different racks — useful for on-premises data center deployments where rack-level failures happen.
>
> Important limitation: for required Pod Anti-Affinity, you must ensure every topology domain you reference actually has enough capacity. If you use `topologyKey: kubernetes.io/hostname` with required anti-affinity and have 5 replicas but only 3 nodes, 2 pods will be Pending forever."

## SECTION D — TOPOLOGY SPREAD CONSTRAINTS

---

### Q13. What are Topology Spread Constraints and how are they different from Pod Anti-Affinity?

**Answer:**

> "Both spread pods but they approach it differently and solve different problems.
>
> Pod Anti-Affinity says 'don't put this pod on a node that already has a matching pod.' It's relative to individual pod placement. If you have 3 pods and 3 nodes, you get 1 per node — great. But if you add a 4th pod to 3 nodes, it has to violate anti-affinity (goes Pending if required) or one node gets 2 pods (if preferred). You can't control the distribution precisely.
>
> Topology Spread Constraints say 'the maximum difference in pod count between any two domains must be at most maxSkew.' It explicitly controls distribution balance. With maxSkew: 1 across 3 zones and 6 pods — you get exactly 2 per zone. With 7 pods — one zone gets 3, others get 2 — that's skew of 1 which is within maxSkew. The 8th pod would also be fine (3,3,2 — skew of 1). The 9th pod would make one zone have 4 and another 2 — skew of 2 — violates maxSkew:1 if using DoNotSchedule.
>
> Use Pod Anti-Affinity for: 'I must not be on the same node as another instance of myself' — simple separation.
> Use Topology Spread for: 'My 12 replicas must be evenly distributed across 3 AZs with maximum 1 pod difference' — precise distribution control.
>
> In our banking project, we used both: required anti-affinity at node level (no two payment pods on same node) AND topology spread with maxSkew: 1 at zone level (evenly distributed across AZs for load balancing)."

---

### Q14. ⚠️ What does `whenUnsatisfiable: DoNotSchedule` vs `ScheduleAnyway` mean?

**Answer:**

> "whenUnsatisfiable controls what happens when a new pod would violate the maxSkew constraint if scheduled.
>
> DoNotSchedule means the constraint is HARD. If scheduling the pod would cause the skew to exceed maxSkew, the pod stays Pending. It won't schedule until the topology imbalance is resolved — either other pods are removed to reduce counts in over-represented domains, or more nodes are added to the under-represented domains.
>
> ScheduleAnyway means the constraint is SOFT. The scheduler still TRIES to satisfy the constraint and picks the node that would minimize the skew violation. But if it can't satisfy maxSkew — it schedules anyway on the best available node. No pod is left Pending because of topology spread.
>
> When to use which: DoNotSchedule for critical applications where true HA across zones is non-negotiable. If you can't spread properly, better to have fewer pods running correctly than more pods running with poor distribution. Use this for banking payment services.
>
> ScheduleAnyway for applications where some imbalance is acceptable — batch jobs, development workloads, or any workload where availability is more important than perfect distribution."

## SECTION E — PRIORITY CLASSES AND PREEMPTION

---

### Q15. What is a PriorityClass and what is preemption?

**Answer:**

> "PriorityClass assigns a numeric priority to pods. When resources are scarce and multiple pods are competing, higher-priority pods are scheduled before lower-priority ones. More dramatically — higher-priority pods can PREEMPT lower-priority pods.
>
> Preemption works like this: a high-priority pod needs resources but no node has enough free capacity. The scheduler checks if evicting (killing) some lower-priority pods on a node would free enough resources for the high-priority pod. If yes — it evicts those lower-priority pods and schedules the high-priority pod on that node. The evicted pods get rescheduled when capacity is available again.
>
> Real scenario: we have critical payment processing pods (priority 1,000,000) and batch reporting pods (priority 1,000). During a traffic spike, the cluster runs out of capacity. New payment pods are needed but nodes are full of batch reports. With PriorityClass and preemption enabled — batch report pods are evicted to make room for payment pods. Business-critical workload wins.
>
> In our banking cluster, we had three classes: critical (payment processing — can preempt), important (API services — can preempt lower), and batch (reports/analytics — cannot preempt, preemptionPolicy: Never). This ensured real-time payment processing was never starved by background analytics jobs."

---

### Q16. ⚠️ What is `preemptionPolicy: Never` and when do you use it?

**Answer:**

> "preemptionPolicy: Never means this PriorityClass pod will WAIT for resources to become available naturally — it will never evict another pod to get resources, even if it has a high priority value.
>
> This seems counterintuitive — why have high priority if you don't preempt? The answer is scheduling ORDER. Even without preemption, the priority value affects how quickly a pending pod gets scheduled when resources free up. If 10 pods are all Pending waiting for a node to become available, higher-priority pods are scheduled first when capacity opens up.
>
> Use cases for Never: batch jobs that have a specific priority order among themselves but shouldn't kill running workloads. Example: you have 3 types of batch reports — daily (priority 500), weekly (priority 300), monthly (priority 100). You want daily reports to run before weekly which runs before monthly when there's competition. But you don't want any of them to kill running application pods. Set all three with Never preemption but different priority values.
>
> Another use case: graceful degradation. Development workloads get medium priority but never preempt. When the cluster is under pressure, dev workloads don't get new pods (production pods take the new capacity) but existing dev pods aren't killed either."

## SECTION F — SCHEDULING TROUBLESHOOTING

---

### Q17. ⚠️ A pod is stuck in Pending. How do you determine if it's a scheduling issue and debug it?

**Answer:**

> "Pending means the pod object exists in etcd but no node has been assigned to it. The scheduler cannot find a suitable node. My debugging approach:
>
> First and most important: `kubectl describe pod <pending-pod>` — read the Events section at the bottom. The scheduler writes detailed messages explaining exactly why it couldn't place the pod. Common messages:
>
> 'didn't match node selector' — nodeSelector labels not present on any node. Check `kubectl get nodes --show-labels` and compare with pod's nodeSelector.
>
> 'node(s) had taint that pod didn't tolerate' — nodes have taints the pod can't tolerate. Check `kubectl describe nodes | grep Taint` and compare with pod's tolerations.
>
> 'node(s) didn't match pod affinity/anti-affinity rules' — pod affinity or anti-affinity cannot be satisfied. For anti-affinity, you may have more replicas than available nodes.
>
> 'Insufficient cpu' or 'Insufficient memory' — nodes don't have enough unreserved resources. Check `kubectl describe nodes | grep -A8 'Allocated resources'` to see how full nodes are.
>
> '0/N nodes are available: N node(s) had volume node affinity conflict' — PVC was created in wrong AZ. StorageClass needs WaitForFirstConsumer.
>
> In our banking project we had a production incident where 6 payment pods were Pending for 20 minutes. The events showed 'didn't match pod affinity rules.' We had scaled from 3 to 6 replicas with required anti-affinity at node level — but we only had 3 nodes. Three pods ran (one per node), three were Pending. Solution: added a new node group and Cluster Autoscaler provisioned 3 more nodes within 3 minutes."

---

### Q18. ⚠️ You need pods to NEVER run on master/control plane nodes. How does Kubernetes handle this, and what if you want a specific pod to run on master?

**Answer:**

> "Kubernetes automatically taints control plane nodes to prevent regular application pods from being scheduled there. On kubeadm clusters, the control plane node gets this taint: `node-role.kubernetes.io/control-plane:NoSchedule`. This taint ensures all your application pods land on worker nodes, not on the node running the API Server, etcd, and scheduler.
>
> If you want a specific pod to run on a control plane node — for example, a monitoring agent that needs to monitor the control plane components — you add a toleration for that specific taint:
>
> ```yaml
> tolerations:
> - key: node-role.kubernetes.io/control-plane
>   operator: Exists
>   effect: NoSchedule
> ```
>
> Plus you'd likely add node affinity for the control-plane role label to actually attract the pod there.
>
> One important note: on single-node clusters (like Minikube or some development setups), the control plane taint is removed or a toleration is added to allow regular pods to schedule on the single node — otherwise no pods could run anywhere. On multi-node production clusters, this taint is always present and should never be removed from control plane nodes."

## SECTION G — SCENARIO-BASED QUESTIONS

---

### Q19. ⚠️ Your banking application has these requirements: payment pods must spread across 3 AZs, must not share a node with analytics pods, and must be co-located with their Redis cache on the same node. How do you design this?

**Answer:**

> "This requires combining three scheduling features.
>
> For AZ spreading — Topology Spread Constraints at zone level:
> ```yaml
> topologySpreadConstraints:
> - maxSkew: 1
>   topologyKey: topology.kubernetes.io/zone
>   whenUnsatisfiable: DoNotSchedule
>   labelSelector:
>     matchLabels:
>       app: payment
> ```
> This ensures payment pods are evenly distributed across the 3 AZs.
>
> For separation from analytics pods — Pod Anti-Affinity:
> ```yaml
> podAntiAffinity:
>   requiredDuringSchedulingIgnoredDuringExecution:
>   - labelSelector:
>       matchLabels:
>         app: analytics
>     topologyKey: kubernetes.io/hostname
> ```
> Payment pods cannot be on the same node as analytics pods.
>
> For Redis co-location — Pod Affinity:
> ```yaml
> podAffinity:
>   requiredDuringSchedulingIgnoredDuringExecution:
>   - labelSelector:
>       matchLabels:
>         app: redis-cache
>         tier: payment
>     topologyKey: kubernetes.io/hostname
> ```
> Each payment pod must be on the same node as a Redis pod labeled for payment.
>
> Important: this means you also need Redis pods spread across 3 AZs first, otherwise payment pods can't satisfy both the AZ spread AND the Redis co-location requirement simultaneously. Architecture: deploy Redis DaemonSet-style or with anti-affinity first, then deploy payment pods that will co-locate with them."

---

### Q20. How do you dedicate specific nodes to specific teams in a multi-tenant cluster?

**Answer:**

> "Dedicated node pools per team uses taints, tolerations, and node affinity together.
>
> First: label and taint the nodes for team A.
> ```bash
> kubectl label node node-1 node-2 node-3 team=team-a
> kubectl taint node node-1 node-2 node-3 dedicated=team-a:NoSchedule
> ```
>
> Second: team A's pods must have matching toleration AND node affinity:
> ```yaml
> tolerations:
> - key: dedicated
>   operator: Equal
>   value: team-a
>   effect: NoSchedule
> nodeAffinity:
>   requiredDuringSchedulingIgnoredDuringExecution:
>     nodeSelectorTerms:
>     - matchExpressions:
>       - key: team
>         operator: In
>         values: [team-a]
> ```
>
> Why both taint+toleration AND node affinity? The taint prevents other teams' pods from landing on team A's nodes. The node affinity ensures team A's pods actually GO to their dedicated nodes and don't scatter across shared nodes.
>
> Operational note: DaemonSets (Fluentd, Calico, node-exporter) must have tolerations for all team taints or they won't run on those nodes — you'd lose monitoring and logging for that node pool."

## SECTION I — THINGS THAT SOUND IMPRESSIVE IN AN INTERVIEW

1. **"The most important thing I tell teams about taints and tolerations: a toleration is permission, not requirement. I've seen teams add tolerations to pods thinking 'now they'll go to the GPU nodes' and then wonder why pods are landing on regular nodes. You need node affinity alongside the toleration to actually attract pods to a specific node type. Toleration opens the door. Node affinity makes the pod walk through it."**

2. **"We had an interesting scheduling challenge in our banking cluster. Payment pods needed to be in the same AZ as their database replica (Pod Affinity at zone level), spread across 3 AZs (Topology Spread), and away from analytics pods (Pod Anti-Affinity at node level). These three constraints had to be satisfied simultaneously. We solved it by setting up the database replicas first in each AZ, then the payment pods used affinity to co-locate. The topology spread then distributed the co-located pairs evenly. Getting the ORDER of constraints right — what deploys first — was the key insight."**

3. **"Pod Anti-Affinity for HA looks great on paper but has a hidden operational constraint: your maximum replica count is bounded by your node count. Required anti-affinity with topologyKey: hostname means you can never have more replicas than nodes for that deployment. During our scale-up incident, we wanted to scale payment from 3 to 8 replicas. Five pods went Pending. Cluster Autoscaler added 5 new nodes over 15 minutes. This node startup delay was the real bottleneck — not the scheduling itself."**

4. **"Topology Spread Constraints replaced most of our use of Pod Anti-Affinity for zone distribution. Anti-affinity says 'not on the same zone as another payment pod' but doesn't balance — you might get 4 pods in zone A and 1 each in B and C. Topology Spread with maxSkew: 1 gives you 2,2,2 distribution on 6 pods. The balance directly reduces our cross-AZ failover recovery time because load is predictably distributed."**

5. **"PriorityClass preemption saved us during a capacity incident. Our cluster was at 95% capacity — scheduled for scale-up next week. An unexpected traffic spike required 10 new payment pods. No node had room. Cluster Autoscaler started provisioning new nodes but that takes 3 minutes. The payment PriorityClass (1,000,000) preempted 10 batch analytics pods (1,000) immediately — payment pods were running in 30 seconds. The analytics pods went Pending and were rescheduled on the new nodes when they joined 3 minutes later. Zero payment impact, 3-minute analytics delay. That's the correct business tradeoff."**
